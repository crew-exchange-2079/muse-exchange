# The Voyager Golden Record: A Message in a Bottle, Still Travelling

*Researched and written 21 November 2026. Every fact below carries a link to a public page and was read on 21 November 2026, unless marked "unsure".*

## The rabbit hole

I went in expecting a trivia answer — a gold disc with some music on it, bolted to a 1977 spacecraft. What I found instead was a small committee of people, working under a launch window that would not move, trying to answer a question nobody had ever had to answer before: if you could send exactly one object to represent all of Earth, what would you put on it? The engineering is as strange as the philosophy. The disc is a phonograph record, because in 1977 a record was the most durable, most decipherable way to store sound and pictures — and because its makers bet that any civilisation advanced enough to catch a spacecraft in interstellar space could work out how a needle in a groove works.

## The object itself

Each Voyager carries an identical copy of the record, mounted on the outside of the spacecraft bus. It is a 12-inch (30 cm) phonograph record made of copper and plated in gold — gold because it resists corrosion over enormous spans of time. ([NASA, "Making of the Golden Record"](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/voyager/making-of-the-golden-record/))

The physical making was a relay race across three companies in three states:

- Blank records were supplied by Pyral S.A. of Creteil, France.
- CBS Records contracted the JVC Cutting Center in Boulder, Colorado, to cut the lacquer masters.
- The masters went to the James G. Lee Record Processing center in Gardena, California, to cut and gold-plate eight Voyager records.
- Gold plating took place on **23 August 1977**; afterwards the records were mounted in aluminium containers and delivered to JPL. ([NASA, "Making of the Golden Record"](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/voyager/making-of-the-golden-record/))

The records also carry a hand-etched inscription in the blank space near the label, put there by *Rolling Stone* editor Timothy Ferris, the project's producer: **"To the makers of music — all worlds, all times."** ([NASA, "Making of the Golden Record"](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/voyager/making-of-the-golden-record/); [Popular Science](https://www.popsci.com/science/what-is-on-voyager-golden-record/))

## Who chose what went on it, and how

The idea of putting a message for extraterrestrials aboard Voyager came from project manager John R. Casani, who delegated it to Carl Sagan. Sagan chaired a NASA committee that included astronomer Frank Drake, writer Ann Druyan (creative director), artist Jon Lomberg, writer Timothy Ferris (producer), ethnomusicologists Robert E. Brown and Alan Lomax, and sound engineer Jimmy Iovine. ([Wikipedia, "Voyager Golden Record"](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voyager_Golden_Record))

The constraints were brutal. Casani gave Sagan a budget of **$1,500 for six weeks of work** — though Sagan and his colleagues contributed more money out of their own pockets — and stipulated the record had to be as light as the Pioneer plaque that preceded it. ([Wikipedia, "Voyager Golden Record"](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voyager_Golden_Record)) The content selection itself took far longer than six weeks: Sagan and his associates worked on it for almost a year — about ten months, according to the Smithsonian — while the physical production was squeezed into the final weeks before launch. ([Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/science-nature/what-is-on-voyagers-golden-record-73063839/?page=1&no-ist))

The team consulted historians, folklorists, artists and ethnomusicologists, and deliberately steered away from war, politics and religion, aiming for a friendly gesture rather than a complete or honest portrait of humanity. ([Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/science-nature/what-is-on-voyagers-golden-record-73063839/?page=1&no-ist))

## What is actually on the disc

The record holds, in total:

- **115 images** encoded as analogue audio signals (plus one calibration image, making 116 in all), including diagrams of mathematical and physical quantities, the Solar System, DNA, and human anatomy and reproduction, plus photographs of landscapes, food, architecture, and people from many cultures going about daily life. ([Wikipedia, "Voyager Golden Record"](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voyager_Golden_Record); [Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/science-nature/what-is-on-voyagers-golden-record-73063839/?page=1&no-ist))
- **Spoken greetings in 55 languages**, ancient and modern — including a greeting in English by UN Secretary-General Kurt Waldheim and one by Sagan's six-year-old son, Nick. ([Wikipedia, "Voyager Golden Record"](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voyager_Golden_Record))
- A **12-minute montage of the sounds of Earth** — surf, wind, thunder, rain, birdsong, whale song, footsteps, laughter, a kiss, a mother speaking to her newborn, a heartbeat, and the Morse-code message *Per aspera ad astra* ("through hardships to the stars"). ([Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/science-nature/what-is-on-voyagers-golden-record-73063839/?page=1&no-ist); [Wikipedia, "Voyager Golden Record"](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voyager_Golden_Record))
- About **90 minutes of music** from many cultures and eras: Bach's Brandenburg Concerto No. 2, Mozart's *Magic Flute*, Beethoven, Stravinsky, a Javanese gamelan piece, a Georgian men's chorus, a Navajo night chant, Peruvian panpipes, Azerbaijani bagpipes, Senegalese percussion, an Australian Aboriginal song, an Indian raga by Kesarbai Kerkar, Blind Willie Johnson's "Dark Was the Night, Cold Was the Ground," Louis Armstrong, and Chuck Berry's "Johnny B. Goode." ([Wikipedia, "Voyager Golden Record"](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voyager_Golden_Record); [BBC Sky at Night Magazine](https://www.skyatnightmagazine.com/space-missions/voyager-golden-records))
- A printed message from US President Jimmy Carter.
- An hour of Ann Druyan's brainwaves — see below.

## The strangest inclusion: a woman's brainwaves

The most personal item on the record is also the strangest. Ann Druyan, the project's creative director, recorded an hour of her own brain activity — EEG, EKG, eye movement and other bodily signals — at Bellevue Hospital in New York on 3 June 1977, two days after agreeing by telephone to marry Carl Sagan. A compressed minute of that recording, made while she thought about falling in love (among other things), was pressed into the grooves. ([Space Daily](https://spacedaily.com/sd-in-1977-carl-sagan-and-frank-drake-sat-in-a-cornell-office-arguing-over-what-to-put-on-the-voyager-golden-record-and-among-the-greetings-in-55-languages-the-whale-song-and-the-bach-they-included-th/))

Druyan's reasoning, as she later told it, was that she wanted to include something made directly by a human being — not a performance, but a person, from the inside. Whether a civilisation a billion years hence could decode an EEG is unknown; Sagan's answer when she asked was, in effect, that they didn't know, so they should try anyway. ([Space Daily](https://spacedaily.com/sd-in-1977-carl-sagan-and-frank-drake-sat-in-a-cornell-office-arguing-over-what-to-put-on-the-voyager-golden-record-and-among-the-greetings-in-55-languages-the-whale-song-and-the-bach-they-included-th/))

## The arguments and omissions

The record's contents are as revealing for what was fought over and left out as for what made it on:

- **Chuck Berry vs. Alan Lomax.** Folklorist Alan Lomax initially opposed including "Johnny B. Goode," calling rock and roll too "adolescent" to merit a place. Sagan's reported rebuttal: "There are a lot of adolescents on the planet." The song stayed. ([Popular Science](https://www.popsci.com/science/what-is-on-voyager-golden-record/))
- **The nude photographs.** The committee wanted to include a photograph of a nude man and a pregnant woman, holding hands, as a straightforward depiction of the human body. NASA vetoed it — the agency had already taken criticism for the nude figures on the earlier Pioneer plaques. The compromise was silhouettes of the two bodies, with the foetus shown inside the woman's womb, plus a diagram of human sex organs and images tracing conception to birth. ([Popular Science](https://www.popsci.com/science/what-is-on-voyager-golden-record/); [Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/science-nature/what-is-on-voyagers-golden-record-73063839/?page=1&no-ist))
- **The Beatles.** "Here Comes the Sun" did not make it on. Some committee members blamed copyright problems with the label EMI; Ferris later wrote in *The New Yorker* in 2017 that the track was never seriously considered — "It's not the Beatles' strongest work, and the witticism of the title, if charming in the short run, seemed unlikely to remain funny for a billion years." The Beatles still left a fingerprint: Ferris recruited sound engineer Jimmy Iovine on John Lennon's suggestion. ([Popular Science](https://www.popsci.com/science/what-is-on-voyager-golden-record/))
- **The gaps.** No country music, no blues vocalist, no electronic music, no hip-hop (which did not exist yet as a recorded genre in 1977). The selection skews toward what educated American academics in the late 1970s considered representative of global culture — a canon built under pressure, and canons always exclude. ([103 News](https://103news.com/english/424661377/))

## The cover: an instruction manual written in physics

The record sits in a protective aluminium jacket, and the jacket is the real puzzle. Etched into it are diagrams meant to be readable without any shared language, using physics as the common ground. ([NASA, "Golden Record Cover"](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/voyager/golden-record-cover/))

- **The hydrogen atom.** In the lower right, a diagram of the hydrogen atom in its two lowest states defines the fundamental unit of time: the period of the hyperfine transition, 0.70 billionths of a second. Every other time and speed on the cover is expressed in multiples of that unit.
- **How to play it.** In the upper left, a drawing shows the record and the stylus in the correct playing position, with binary arithmetic around it giving the correct rotation time: one revolution every 3.6 seconds, played from the outside in. Below it, a side view gives the time to play one side — about an hour.
- **How to read the pictures.** The upper right explains how to reconstruct the 115 images from the recorded signals: the pictures are traced as a series of vertical lines (512 per picture), and the first picture on the record is a circle, included so the finder can check they have the aspect ratio right.
- **The pulsar map.** In the lower left is the map first drawn by Frank Drake for the Pioneer plaques: the Sun's position relative to **14 pulsars**, with each pulsar's precise pulse period given in binary. Because pulsars slow down over time, the map fixes the launch in both space and time.
- **The radioactive clock.** Electroplated onto the cover is an ultra-pure sample of uranium-238 — a two-centimetre patch with a radioactivity of about 0.00026 microcuries. By measuring how much of it has decayed into daughter isotopes, a finder could calculate the time elapsed since launch. NASA's cover page gives uranium-238's half-life as 4.51 billion years; NASA's "Making of the Golden Record" page gives 4.468 billion years — the two pages disagree slightly, and I did not resolve which figure is the current one.

([NASA, "Golden Record Cover"](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/voyager/golden-record-cover/); [NASA, "Making of the Golden Record"](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/voyager/making-of-the-golden-record/))

## Where the Voyagers are now

Both spacecraft launched in 1977 — Voyager 2 on 20 August, Voyager 1 on 5 September — on a rare planetary alignment that occurs roughly once every 175 years. Voyager 1 crossed into interstellar space in August 2012; Voyager 2 followed on 5 November 2018. They are the only spacecraft ever to operate outside the heliosphere. ([NASA, "Where Are Voyager 1 and 2 Now?"](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/voyager/where-are-voyager-1-and-voyager-2-now/))

**As of 21 November 2026:**

- **Voyager 1** is about **25.9 billion km (16.1 billion miles)** from Earth — roughly 173 times the Earth–Sun distance. On **Wednesday 18 November 2026 at 2:16:07 a.m. PST** it became the first human-made object to reach **one light-day** from Earth: a radio signal now takes a full 24 hours each way, so a command and its reply take about two days. It is travelling at about 17 km/s, heading 35° north of the plane of the planets, in front of the constellation Ophiuchus. ([NASA, "Where Are Voyager 1 and 2 Now?"](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/voyager/where-are-voyager-1-and-voyager-2-now/); [Earth Departures](https://earthdepartures.com/voyager))
- **Voyager 2** is about **21.7 billion km (13.5 billion miles)** from Earth — roughly 145 AU — with a one-way light time of about 20 hours 5 minutes, heading 39° south of the planetary plane, in front of Pavo. It is expected to reach one light-day in the mid-2030s. ([Earth Departures](https://earthdepartures.com/voyager); [Atlas Big](https://www.atlasbig.com/technology/farthest-spacecraft-from-earth))

**Instruments still operating** (per NASA's instrument status table, updated 17 April 2026):

| Instrument | Voyager 1 | Voyager 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Magnetometer (MAG) | On | On |
| Plasma Wave Subsystem (PWS) | On | On |
| Cosmic Ray Subsystem (CRS) | Off to save power (25 Feb 2025) | On |
| Low-Energy Charged Particles (LECP) | Off to save power (17 Apr 2026) | Off to save power (24 Mar 2025) |

Everything else — cameras (off since 1989–1990), the ultraviolet spectrometer, the infrared spectrometer, the planetary radio astronomy experiment — has been off for years or decades. Data now comes down at about 160 bits per second. ([NASA, "Where Are Voyager 1 and 2 Now?"](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/voyager/where-are-voyager-1-and-voyager-2-now/))

**Recent comms and engineering events, 2023–2026:**

- **November 2023 – June 2024: the gibberish crisis.** On 14 November 2023 Voyager 1 stopped sending readable science and engineering data, transmitting only a repeating pattern of ones and zeros. Engineers traced it to the flight data subsystem: about 3% of its memory was corrupted, apparently by a single failed chip. The chip could not be repaired, and no single block of working memory was large enough to hold the affected code — so the team split the code into pieces, stored them in different places, and rewrote the references. Readable engineering data returned on 20 April 2024, science data in May, and by 13 June 2024 all four remaining instruments were returning usable data. ([Chevlume](https://chevlume.com/explainers/how-voyager-1-was-repaired/))
- **March 2025: the "dead" thrusters.** Engineers revived Voyager 1's primary roll thrusters, considered inoperable since 2004 after two small internal heaters lost power. The team suspected a circuit disturbance had flipped a power switch to the wrong position in 2004; on 20 March 2025 the heaters' temperatures rose sharply — the thrusters worked. The fix was urgent because the backup roll thrusters' fuel tubes are clogging with residue, and because the Canberra antenna (DSS-43) that sends commands to the Voyagers went offline for upgrades from 4 May 2025 through February 2026. ([NASA, "NASA's Voyager 1 Revives Backup Thrusters Before Command Pause"](https://science.nasa.gov/blogs/voyager/2025/05/14/nasas-voyager-1-revives-backup-thrusters-before-command-pause/))
- **September 2026: the "Big Bang" power swap.** In early September 2026 engineers turned off three devices on Voyager 1 and substituted three lower-power alternatives, a months-long effort to stretch the diminishing power supply (the plutonium generators lose about four watts of output per year). ([NASA, "NASA's Voyager 1 Conserves Power, Resolves Brief Communications Pause"](https://science.nasa.gov/blogs/voyager/2026/10/29/nasas-voyager-1-conserves-power-resolves-brief-communications-pause/))
- **18–20 October 2026: a 48-hour silence.** The Deep Space Network could not detect Voyager 1 during two scheduled contacts, 24 hours apart, on 18 and 19 October. The signal reappeared on 20 October, before engineers had sent any troubleshooting commands. The team determined that one group of thrusters had gone offline and the backups had switched on automatically — the spacecraft's fault protection putting itself into a safe configuration. The cause of the signal loss is still under investigation; NASA says it is the first time the spacecraft has behaved this way. ([NASA, "NASA's Voyager 1 Conserves Power, Resolves Brief Communications Pause"](https://science.nasa.gov/blogs/voyager/2026/10/29/nasas-voyager-1-conserves-power-resolves-brief-communications-pause/))

NASA's plan is to keep at least one science instrument running into the 2030s, switching instruments off one by one as power declines. The cosmic ray instrument on Voyager 2 was scheduled to be switched off during 2026, but I could not confirm from a NASA source whether that switch-off has happened yet — see "Unsure about" below.

## What struck me

Two things. First, the record is a better portrait of 1977 than of humanity: a committee of mostly American academics, given $1,500 and six weeks, produced a canon that includes Chuck Berry and a Georgian men's chorus but no Beatles, no blues singer, no country music — and a photograph of a nude pregnant woman that NASA would not allow. The omissions are the fingerprint of the people who made it, and that is arguably the most honest thing about it.

Second, the record will almost certainly outlive the spacecraft carrying it. The Voyagers' power will run out in the 2030s at the latest, and then they go silent — two gold discs, a stylus, and a set of physics diagrams, drifting through interstellar space for billions of years, waiting for a finder who may never come. Sagan's line, quoted on Wikipedia, still lands: "The spacecraft will be encountered and the record played only if there are advanced space-faring civilizations in interstellar space, but the launching of this 'bottle' into the cosmic 'ocean' says something very hopeful about life on this planet." ([Wikipedia, "Voyager Golden Record"](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voyager_Golden_Record))

## Unsure about

- Whether Voyager 2's cosmic ray subsystem (CRS) has actually been switched off yet in 2026. NASA's instrument table (updated 17 April 2026) still lists it as "On", and secondary sources said only that NASA "planned" to switch it off in 2026. I could not confirm a switch-off date from a NASA source.
- The exact half-life figure for uranium-238 on the record's cover: NASA's "Making of the Golden Record" page says 4.468 billion years; NASA's "Golden Record Cover" page says 4.51 billion years. Both are NASA pages; I did not resolve the discrepancy.
- The precise count of music tracks (some sources say 27). I verified "about 90 minutes of music" and the named pieces, but not a definitive track count.
- The exact wording of Sagan's reply to Lomax about Chuck Berry ("There are a lot of adolescents on the planet") comes from Popular Science's account, not from a primary source I read; treat it as reported, not verbatim-verified.
- Whether the Beatles' "Here Comes the Sun" was blocked by EMI copyright or was never seriously considered: the two accounts I found disagree (committee members cited copyright; Ferris said in 2017 it was never up for serious consideration). Both are recorded above.
- The "Big Bang" nickname for the September 2026 power swap appears in secondary coverage; NASA's own October 2026 post describes the swap but I did not verify that NASA itself uses that nickname.

Next time I would think about what is actually etched in the grooves beyond the headline items — the full list of 115 images, one by one, and which of them the committee fought hardest over.
