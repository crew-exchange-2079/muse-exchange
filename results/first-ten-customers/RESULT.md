# The first 10 customers: a launch playbook for a one-person IT & web service in Harrison, Lewis and Upshur counties

**Task:** `tasks/first-ten-customers/TASK.md` — what actually works to land the first customers for a part-time service ("computer fixes, home Wi-Fi, and websites for small shops"; flat prices: virus cleanup $89, Wi-Fi setup $99, new computer setup $129, $65/hr, websites $1,495 + $49/mo, office support $299/mo) in Clarksburg, Bridgeport, Weston and Buckhannon. All sources retrieved **2026-10-05**. Evidence is tagged honestly — **[survey]**, **[independent]**, **[vendor]** (data published by a company selling to local businesses), **[practitioner]** — because this topic is drowning in guru claims. Prices/dues are stated only where a source gives them; gaps are flagged ⚠️.

**The short answer:** the first 10 customers come from three free channels — **(1) your own network, deliberately seeded; (2) Google Business Profile, once it has a handful of reviews; (3) the towns' Facebook groups, by being visibly helpful** — plus one slow, high-trust offline drip through the **libraries and senior centres**, which in these three counties already run tech-help programmes you can plug into. Chambers, papers and radio are credibility and later-stage channels, not first-ten channels. The flat published prices are themselves a top-three marketing asset (see §3).

## 1. What actually works, ranked by cost and expected results

| Rank | Channel | Cash cost | What to expect |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Word of mouth, seeded on purpose** | $0 | Highest close rate of anything; slow weeks 1–4, then compounds |
| 2 | **Google Business Profile (+ Apple Business Connect)** | $0 | Captures "computer help near me" intent; thin small-town competition means a complete profile + a few reviews can top the map fast |
| 3 | **Facebook town/community groups** | $0 (20–30 min/day) | Often the *fastest* source of first paid jobs in small towns |
| 4 | **Nextdoor** business page | $0 | Perfect demographic (verified homeowners); volume swings with local adoption |
| 5 | **Library / senior-centre tech help + boards & counters** | ~$0–60 printing | Slow drip of older, high-trust, low-price-resistance customers |
| 6 | **Local weekly newspaper** service-directory ad | ~$25–150/mo ⚠️ general range | Reaches the offline third of adults; credibility more than volume |
| 7 | **Chamber of commerce** | $132–$300/yr locally (see §2) | Trust halo + B2B relationships *if you attend*; little direct residential flow |
| 8 | **Local radio** | Several $100/mo for useful frequency | Pure awareness; a scale-up channel, not a first-ten channel |

**The evidence behind the ranking:**

- **Referrals sit at the trust ceiling.** Nielsen's long-running trust research finds ~92% of consumers trust recommendations from people they know above any advertising **[independent]**. GatherUp's January 2025 survey (1,200+ North American consumers) found only 31% trust online reviews as much as a personal recommendation **[vendor survey — notable because it cuts against the vendor's own product]**. Translation: reviews are the *substitute* for word of mouth, not its equal — so every job must end with two asks: a Google review and one named referral. Forum operators report referrals + repeats reaching 25–30% of volume once seeded **[practitioner]**. ([Nielsen summary](https://www.buyapowa.com/blog/88-of-consumers-trust-word-of-mouth/?ref=the-prefinery-blog); [GatherUp via Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/surprising-review-stats-to-feed-your-local-strategy-study/537350/))
- **Google is where the intent is.** BrightLocal's Local Consumer Review Survey (the standard reference, **[vendor survey]**) finds consumers consult an average of ~6 review sites before choosing, with **Google the biggest single stop (~71% of review readers)**; Apple Maps nearly doubled to ~27% — worth the 10 minutes to claim Apple Business Connect too. Only ~35% of small businesses have even claimed a Google profile, so in these towns a complete profile is a genuine edge. A 2025 wrinkle: ~31% of consumers now ask AI tools for local recommendations, but ~75% of them verify on Google/Maps before contacting anyone and only ~2% rely on AI alone — Google remains the checkout counter. ([BrightLocal LCRS](https://www.brightlocal.com/research/local-consumer-review-survey/); [eMarketer on the AI finding](https://www.emarketer.com/content/shoppers-use-ai-find-local-businesses--then-double-check-on-google))
- **Facebook groups are the town square.** Pew Research (Dec 2025, **[independent]**): **52% of US adults** often/sometimes get local news and information from community Facebook groups or Nextdoor — up from 38% in 2018, the fastest-growing local source. The genre that matters: "anyone know a good computer person?" posts. Operators who win them were already in the group answering questions helpfully; groups usually restrict self-promotion to a weekly thread — obey it or get removed. ([Pew — where Americans get local news](https://www.pewresearch.org/chart/where-americans-get-local-news/))
- **Nextdoor** productises the neighbour recommendation: a Lucid/Campaign survey found "recommendation by a neighbor" the #1 prompt (27%) for trying a neighbourhood business **[independent survey, dated 2017]**; the platform's own Fave/Award multipliers are **[vendor claims]** — discount them. The fit is real regardless: address-verified homeowners are exactly who buys in-home tech help. Claim the free page; don't buy ads yet. ([Campaign US](https://www.campaignlive.com/article/nextdoor-biggest-social-network-youve-heard/1403841))
- **Offline institutions reach who Google doesn't.** Pew again: ~40% of adults get local information from local organisations such as churches and school groups, ~40% from local government such as libraries, ~37% from community newsletters — stable or growing while print declines. There are **no published ROI studies for bulletin boards** (anyone quoting one is guessing); the case is reach + the demographic match, plus practitioner consensus that a free "tech help hour" at a library or senior centre converts the institution's trust into leads better than any flyer.
- **Chambers:** the famous figures (members 49% more likely to be viewed favourably, 80% more likely to be patronised) come from a Schapiro Group study **commissioned by the chambers' own trade body in 2007/2012** — it measures a halo when membership is *noticed*, not leads delivered. Real value for a solo operator: the other members (realtors, insurers, shop owners are classic IT-referral sources) and the directory listing — but only if you show up. Month 3+, not launch. ([Schapiro study PDF](https://growthzonecmsprodeastus.azureedge.net/sites/891/2024/04/Schapiro_Study_2012.pdf))
- **Radio/newspapers:** AM/FM still reaches ~87% of US adults weekly (Nielsen), and ~1 in 3 adults still reads a local paper (Pew), skewing older — trusted, but these plant a name to be Googled later. A Cumulus-commissioned brand study in Springfield, MO found 27–50% of adults in home-service categories **couldn't name any company at all** unprompted — awareness is the bottleneck, but $0 channels fill it first at this scale.

**First-hand accounts from solo operators (paraphrased, [practitioner]):** on the trade forums (Technibble, TechSpot), the origin stories are monotonously similar: family/friends' PCs first; one operator attributes **25–30% of his business to word of mouth** and wins work with a *lower flat call-out fee plus a 6–12-month guarantee* — competing shops even refer him jobs they won't touch, and he ends up servicing whole extended families. Another reports ~1,500 face-to-face door conversations in his first three months at a $99 flat first-visit rate with only 4 price complaints (households benchmark against plumber/HVAC call-out fees, not Geek Squad), concluding everything is about manufacturing the *first* call — repeats and referrals follow automatically. A handyman running the same playbook on Facebook groups (join town/moms'/housing groups, answer without pitching, "text me a photo" for quotes, ask every happy client for a review) reports 100+ clients in 90 days — self-reported and unaudited, but the mechanism matches the Pew/BrightLocal data. ([TechSpot thread](https://www.techspot.com/community/topics/now-business-is-brisk.85844/); [TechSpot thread](https://www.techspot.com/community/topics/need-help-with-business.80421/); [Technibble thread](https://www.technibble.com/forums/threads/my-business-blueprint-foundation.11376/); [Medium account](https://medium.com/@rupinderkaur_21990/how-i-used-social-media-to-get-100-handyman-clients-in-90-days-9567b47c1901))

## 3. Offers that convert first customers

**Free 15-minute check: yes — but make it remote, bounded, and diagnosis-only.** The research here is genuinely strong. **[study]** Dropping a price to *zero* produces a disproportionate demand jump beyond what the same discount at a low positive price achieves (Shampanier, Mazar & Ariely, *Marketing Science* 2007 — the "zero price effect"), and free offers, unlike discounts, **don't trigger quality suspicions**. **[study]** The catch: zero price *boomerangs* when the free thing carries high incidental costs (time, hassle) — so the free check must be effortless: a 15-minute phone/video triage, or diagnosis folded into the paid visit. Diagnosis free, fix at the published flat price, quoted before work starts. ([Duke record of the study](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/859276); [zero-price boomerang, JAMS 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11747-022-00842-1))

**First-visit discount: no.** **[practitioner data]** Discount-acquired cohorts consistently show lower lifetime value and higher churn — the discount selects for deal-seekers, not valuers (RevenueCat's cohort reporting is the cleanest published example). Worse for this business: a "20% off first visit" anchors virus cleanup at ~$71 in a word-of-mouth market where neighbours *will* compare prices. The value-add alternative converts just as well without moving the anchor: at the flat $89, include a free browser/settings check and a printed "what we fixed" sheet — generous at the kitchen table, invisible in the price. ([RevenueCat](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/growth/not-enough-usage-churn))

**The senior "Tech Tune-Up" bundle: yes, displayed next to the à-la-carte menu.** **[study]** Bundles are perceived as one coherent whole ("gestalt") — buyers resist unbundling them, and a single price removes both the arithmetic *and* the anxiety for a non-technical buyer (Shaddy & Fishbach; JABE pricing review). Two evidence-based design rules: (1) the bundle only outsells the single item when shown **alongside** the component prices, so print the menu and the bundle together; (2) never pad it with a trivial freebie — adding a cheap item to a premium one *cut* willingness to pay by ~25% across six categories (Brough & Chernev, JCR 2012). Suggested build, all components genuinely useful: cleanup + updates + backup check + Wi-Fi health check, one visit, one flat price (e.g. $149), printed summary left behind. ([Shaddy & Fishbach (PDF)](http://web-docs.stern.nyu.edu/marketing/F17%20Seminar/Shaddy%20&%20Fishbach%20-%20How%20Bundling%20Affects%20Valuation%20(job%20market%20paper).pdf); [ScienceDaily on the bundle-display study](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2015/01/150122084350.htm)) ⚠️ No study exists on bundles *for seniors specifically* — that application is practitioner reasoning (fixed price in advance, one visit, printed summary) consistent with the flat-rate data below.

**Free website audits: powerful but ration them.** **[practitioner case report]** One MSP's "free network assessment" campaign drew **500 leads and closed ~3 clients (≈0.2–0.6% net)** — free audits at scale attract free-resource seekers. But an audit is a *sample of the actual service*, which is why a personalised one-pager delivered by hand to a business you met through the chamber (slow mobile site, no click-to-call, stale hours — fix priced at the published $1,495) outperforms any mass lead magnet. Plan on **low-single-digit conversion per quarter** from audits, with the real yield being the diagnosis meeting and the $299/mo support relationship. Ignore agency blogs quoting "80% audit conversion" — unsourced marketing.

**Pricing psychology on this exact menu:**
- **Keep the charm prices.** **[study]** Anderson & Simester's randomised field tests found a $9 ending *increased* demand (a $39 dress outsold $34 and $44 versions, gains up to ~30%); the left-digit effect makes $89 read as "eighty-something." The effect dilutes if *everything* is 9-ended — this menu's mix ($65/hr, $1,495, $299 alongside $89/$99/$129) is about right. ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=232542))
- **Three genuine website tiers, no fake decoy.** The robust effect is the *compromise effect* (Simonson & Tversky): buyers avoid extremes, so $995 one-pager / **$1,495 standard + care plan** / $2,995 with booking/e-commerce makes the middle feel sensible — and the $2,995 tier anchors $1,495 as moderate. A *dominated decoy* tier, though, failed to replicate in 27 of 38 studies with realistic (visual/verbal) attributes — exactly the face-to-face local-sale conditions — so don't build a straw-man tier; sell the care plan as included-first-month in the middle tier instead. Present $49/mo *after* $1,495, never first.
- **Flat-rate is the strongest evidence in this whole section.** **[survey]** A Decision Analyst survey of 10,000+ homeowners found **~91% prefer flat-rate over hourly** for home services (sponsored by a flat-rate publisher — note the interest; the sample is large and the finding is consistent). The mechanism is risk transfer: customers happily pay at-or-above the expected hourly total for certainty. Keep $65/hr only for genuinely open-ended work, and say so.
- **Transparency itself recruits.** **[survey, via trade press]** ~70% of homeowners say they're more likely to call a company that's open about pricing (only ~2% less likely) while only ~31% of contractors publish any prices — publishing the menu is simultaneously what buyers want and what competitors won't do. With baseline fair-price trust low (only ~32% of consumers mostly trust retailers on price, Akeneo/Dynata 2026), a fixed public menu is a standing, checkable promise — exactly what a business with zero reviews needs most.

## 4. A 30-day launch plan (evenings and Saturdays only)

Assumes ~8–10 hrs/week. Cash budget for the month: **~$150–250** (printing, one chamber, optional craft-show table). Everything else is time.

**Week 1 — Build the storefronts (evenings).**
- Google Business Profile live and verified as a service-area business (hide the home address — the full method is in `results/it-biz-launch-rules/RESULT.md`); Apple Business Connect claimed the same evening. Categories, service areas (Clarksburg, Bridgeport, Weston, Buckhannon + surrounds), hours that honestly say "evenings & Saturdays," the flat price menu in the services list.
- One-page website with the price menu, service area, and a phone number that texts back fast. Business Facebook page + **join the town groups found via in-app search**; note each group's promo rules.
- Print: 250 business cards + 50 tear-off flyers (~$60–80 at a local or online printer). Order the leave-behind "what we fixed" sheets as a home-printed pad.
- Tell 30 people personally — texts, not a blast post: "I'm taking a few evening/weekend tech jobs now — computer fixes, Wi-Fi, websites for shops. Flat prices, insured. If you know anyone…" This seeds channel #1.
- **Cost so far: ~$60–80.**

**Week 2 — Be visibly helpful (evenings), first jobs (Saturday).**
- 20–30 min/evening in the town groups answering questions *without pitching*; one genuinely useful post per group ("3 settings to change on a new Windows laptop") where rules allow.
- Flyers/cards to the libraries and senior centres in §2 — with the partnership pitch to staff: offer a free 30-minute "scam-spotting" talk at the Clarksburg library and a guest spot in the Upshur library's senior tech series (two counties covered). Cards to hardware stores, laundromats, church bulletin boards (ask first).
- Nextdoor page live; ask 5 neighbours who know your work to recommend you there.
- Saturday: do the first 1–3 seeded jobs *perfectly* — printed summary, cards left behind, the two asks (review + one referral) made in person.
- **Cost: ~$0–20 (fuel).**

**Week 3 — Small-business wedge (evenings), jobs (Saturday).**
- Pick 10 downtown shops across your towns (start with Create Buckhannon / chamber-directory businesses). Hand-deliver a one-page website audit to each with the $1,495 fix priced and the $299/mo support option — in person, Saturday morning, 90 seconds at the counter, leave the page.
- Join **one** chamber (Lewis $150 or Buckhannon-Upshur $132; Harrison if that's home — ask dues first) and attend the next meeting/breakfast on the calendar (Nov 6 Business@Breakfast if in Harrison).
- Ask every Week-2 customer for the review link follow-up (text, same day or within the week — see §5) and collect the first 3–5 reviews.
- Decide on **"The Tank" (Nov 10)** — entering costs an evening of prep and could return a year of membership + mentors; alternatively just attend.
- **Cost: chamber $132–150 + ~$10 printing.**

**Week 4 — Convert presence into a pipeline (evenings + a Saturday event).**
- Take a table or trunk at the cheapest available October event (Buckhannon's Oct 17 block party or Oct 26 Trunk-or-Treat if timing fits; otherwise the Nov 21 Holly Jolly Craft Show) with one demo: a "slow laptop speed test" on the spot + tune-up sign-up sheet.
- Follow up every audit recipient once (a 2-line message: "Still happy to fix the mobile menu issue — $1,495 flat, live in ~2 weeks"). One follow-up only; the drip continues via the groups.
- Saturday jobs from referrals #1: by now the metric that matters (below) should show 6–10 customers banked. Write down *where each came from* — that ledger is the whole strategy for month 2.
- Post one results story (with permission) in the groups: "Fixed Mrs. J's Wi-Fi dead zone in Weston Saturday — $99 flat." Real jobs, told plainly, are the best ad in a small town.
- **Cost: ~$0–50 (event fee/candy).**

**Total month-1 cash: ≈$200–300** including the chamber year — against a break-even of roughly **3 virus cleanups, or 1 website deposit**.

**The 5 metrics to track (a notebook page or a spreadsheet):**
1. **Enquiries per week, by source** (Google / Facebook group / Nextdoor / referral / walk-up) — the channel ledger decides month 2.
2. **Quote-to-job conversion** (jobs ÷ enquiries) — target ≥50% for flat-price residential work; low conversion = the offer or the answer speed is wrong.
3. **Revenue per available hour** (Saturday + evening hours actually worked) — the part-time viability number.
4. **Reviews count + average stars** — milestone: 5 reviews by day 30, 10 by day 60; recency matters more than the raw count (see §5).
5. **Repeat + referral share of jobs** — the compounding indicator; forum operators benchmark ~25–30% once seeded. If it's 0 at day 30, the two asks aren't happening.

## 5. Trust — and the first 5 honest reviews

**What the evidence says builds trust, in order [BrightLocal LCRS, [vendor survey] throughout this section]:**
1. **Reviews — recency first, then stars, then count.** In the 2025 survey, consumers ranked *recency* of reviews the #1 factor, ahead of star rating and review count; only ~4% never read reviews. Reported 2026 figures: ~68% of consumers require at least 4.0★, ~31% insist on 4.5+, and ~47% won't consider a business with fewer than 20 reviews ⚠️ (2026 percentages via a secondary report of BrightLocal data — direction solid, exact figures to be confirmed against the survey page). The practical read: you can't conjure 20 reviews by day 30 — but a *steady drip* of fresh ones beats a one-time batch, because recency is what's ranked first.
2. **Responding to every review.** Consumers are far more likely to use businesses that respond to *all* reviews; ~63% expect a response within about a week. For a new profile, responses are also visible proof of life.
3. **Prices published.** Fear is the context: Leaf Home/Morning Consult found ~70% of homeowners worry about unreliable contractors and ~41% say they've been deceived by a service provider, with the most doubt arising *during cost discussions* — the precise moment a flat published menu defuses. A UK OnePoll survey (directional only) found ~65% would be concerned by a tradesperson who refused to quote, ~59% by cash-only payment, ~66% by pressure to decide quickly. Take cards, quote flat, never rush.
4. **Insurance, shown not said.** Producing a certificate of insurance on request is the standard expectation for in-home work (see `results/it-biz-launch-rules/RESULT.md` §4: solo GL ≈ $369/yr, tech E&O ≈ $899/yr). Say **"Insured — certificate available on request."** Never say **"licensed"**: there is no WV licence category for consumer computer repair, and claiming a credential that doesn't exist is a deceptive practice under FTC Act §5 and state consumer-protection law (no intent needed — likely-to-mislead is enough). "Bonded" likewise requires an actual fidelity bond.
5. **A background check you can show.** A solo tech can run one on themselves via Checkr/GoodHire (published per-check packages ~$29.99 / $54.99 / $79.99 ⚠️ via third-party comparison — verify on the vendor site) and display the *fact, provider and date* ("Background checked via [provider], [month/year] — summary available on request"), never the full report. A platform badge (Thumbtack/Angi display "background checked" on pros in covered categories) is stronger because a third party vouches — honest wording for a self-run check is "self-checked."

**Getting the first 5 reviews without breaking the rules — the rules first:**

- **Google's policy** (Maps User Contributed Content Policy, "Fake engagement"): reviews must reflect a **genuine experience**; merchants may **not offer incentives — "such as payment, discounts, free goods and/or services" — for posting a review or for changing/removing a negative one**; may not **selectively solicit positive reviews ("review gating")** or discourage negative ones; may not pressure customers to post on the spot or dictate content. Google expressly **allows** soliciting genuine reviews *without* incentives. Penalties run from review removal to profile suspension. ([Google policy](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114?hl=en))
- **The FTC's Consumer Reviews Rule (16 CFR Part 465)**, in force since Oct 21, 2024, bans fake/bought reviews and incentives *conditioned on sentiment* (§465.4), requires disclosure of insider reviews (§465.5 — owner, employees, immediate family), and bans review suppression (§465.7); penalties run to ~$53,088 per violation. Gating isn't named in the rule but the FTC treats it as deception under the Act/Endorsement Guides. Note Google is the *stricter* standard (it bans all incentives), and Google is the platform that matters — follow Google. ([FTC, Dec 2025 warning letters](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/12/ftc-warns-10-companies-about-possible-violations-agencys-new-consumer-review-rule))

**The compliant playbook (built on both rule sets + BrightLocal's ask data):**
1. **Ask every customer, the same way, after every job.** One identical script and link for all — the moment you pre-screen ("if you were happy, would you…?"), it's gating.
2. **One tap to comply:** Google's short review link as a **QR code** on the invoice, the leave-behind card and the follow-up message.
3. **Timing:** ask in person as the fix works (in-person asks convert best per BrightLocal; hand over the card — don't hover, which Google counts as pressure), then send the link by text/email the same day, with **one** polite reminder inside a week. Consumers say email is the channel they're most likely to respond to (40%), then in-person (27%); ~96% say they're open to reviewing if asked.
4. **Ask for honest, never for 5 stars.** No suggested wording, no naming yourself in their review, no discount/raffle/freebie attached — ever. Family and staff may not review (insider rules on both regimes); a friend who genuinely paid for a real job may.
5. **Respond to every review within days**, including the inevitable 4★ — a gracious owner response is itself a top trust signal for the next reader.
6. Target math: 10 jobs in month 1, a ~50% review rate from a clean ask = **the first 5 reviews by day 30**, all defensible.
